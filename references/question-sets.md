# Cagan's question sets

Four generations of the same idea: a short list of questions that makes a team name the problem before building anything. Citations `[S<n>]` resolve to `sources.md`.

## 2006: ten questions

Cagan, "Assessing Product Opportunities", SVPG, 13 Dec 2006 (primary) [S14]. Verbatim:

1. Exactly what problem will this solve? (value proposition)
2. For whom do we solve that problem? (target market)
3. How big is the opportunity? (market size)
4. What alternatives are out there? (competitive landscape)
5. Why are we best suited to pursue this? (our differentiator)
6. Why now? (market window)
7. How will we get this product to market? (go-to-market strategy)
8. How will we measure success/make money from this product? (metrics/revenue strategy)
9. What factors are critical to success? (solution requirements)
10. Given the above, what's the recommendation? (go or no-go)

Cagan's commentary [S14]:

- "The hardest question to answer is usually the first": ask most PMs what problem the product solves and "you usually get a rambling list of features and capabilities, rather than a crisp, clear and compelling statement of exactly the problem that's solved."
- Purpose: "either to a) prevent the company from wasting time and money on poor opportunities; or b) for those that are good opportunities, to understand what will be required to succeed."
- Q3: "be conservative and realize that not every opportunity needs to be a billion dollar market."
- Q9: "we're not describing the product here but rather making clear any dependencies or constraints."
- Present and discuss with senior management, ending in a go/no-go. Even when the CEO mandates the work, "now you know what you're up against."
- It replaced the Market Requirements Document, whose purpose was "to describe the opportunity, not the solution."

Attribution: secondary sources assign the same list to *Inspired* 1st ed. (2008) [S11][S12][S13]; the printed book was not checked. An adaptation for internal or IT products ("Exactly what need will this satisfy?", "How will we encourage adoption?") is secondary and not Cagan's text [S13].

## 2012–2013: three questions

Cagan, "The Opportunity Backlog" (2012) and "Lean Canvas vs. Opportunity Assessment" (2013) (primary) [S15][S16]:

1. What problem are we trying to solve? (why are we doing this)
2. Who are we trying to solve this problem for? (target persona)
3. How will we know if we succeed? (what is the outcome we are hoping for)

- Purpose: "to untangle your initial ideas or assumptions about the solution from the problem that this feature is intended to solve… so often our initial attempts at solving the problem don't pan out, but the problem still remains." [S15]
- Opportunities come mainly from the product vision and the product scorecard, "the prioritized business objectives for this team." [S15]
- Use the assessment for ongoing work on an existing product; a canvas suits a new business where distribution, monetization and cost are open [S16].

## 2017: four questions

Cagan, *Inspired* 2nd ed. (2017), ch. 35 "Opportunity Assessment Technique" (primary, O'Reilly preview) [S17]: "The idea is to answer four key questions about the discovery work you are about to undertake":

1. What business objective is this work intended to address? (*Objective*)
2. How will you know if you've succeeded? (*Key results*)
3. What problem will this solve for our customers? (*Customer problem*)
4. What type of customer are we focused on? (*Target market*)

Chapter guidance [S17]:

- **Objective** "should map to one or more of your team's assigned objectives" (e.g. reduce onboarding time, reduce monthly churn).
- **Key results**: "We want to know at the outset what the measure of success is… if we're trying to reduce churn, would a 1 percent improvement be considered excellent or would it be considered a waste of time?" It should map to at least one assigned key result.
- **Customer problem**: "*We want to keep the focus on our customers.*" Everything benefits the company in some way, but this question articulates the customer's problem.
- The rest of this chapter could not be retrieved, including the "Target Market" text; do not attribute detail beyond the above to Cagan.
- Secondary summaries: the technique finds "the underlying issues that must be tackled" and fits "the vast majority of product work, which ranges from a simple optimization to a medium-sized project." [S18]

What changed from ten to four (synthesis, not Cagan's statement): market size, competition, why-us/why-now, go-to-market and go/no-go are dropped because, in the empowered-team model, leadership has already chosen the problem through strategy. The team only links its work to an *assigned* objective and key result. The six dropped questions still apply when *choosing* a new product or market opportunity, which is a leadership task.

## Customer letter and press release

Cagan, "The Customer Letter" (2017) and *Inspired* 2nd ed. ch. 36 (primary) [S19].

- **When**: larger efforts with "multiple reasons, or several customer problems to be solved, or business objectives to be tackled" (such as a redesign), where four questions are not enough.
- **Amazon press release**: the PM writes an imagined press release: "How does this improve the life of our customers? What are the real benefits to them?" It stops teams from slipping "into an enumeration of all the features they plan to build" and keeps them "focused on the outcome, not the output."
- **Nordstrom customer letter** (Cagan's preference, from Walker Lockhart): an imagined letter from a happy customer to the CEO explaining why, plus the CEO's imagined congratulatory reply to the team about the business effect. It "does an even better job of creating the empathy for the customer's current pain."
- **Test**: if readers (team, impacted teams, leadership) "don't see the value after reading then the product manager has more work to do, or perhaps should reconsider the effort."
- **Caveat, Cagan's own**: it validates value only "with your colleagues rather than real customers, so I think of it primarily as a framing technique."

## Which set when

The first two rows are synthesis from the tension across Cagan's eras, not a rule Cagan states; the rows marked "plugin judgment, not in the sources" are this plugin's own. In 2006 the PM judges whether an opportunity is worth pursuing [S14]; in 2017–2026 problem *selection* moves to product leadership and PMs are warned against gatekeeping [S5][S22].

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| A leader or strategist choosing which problems to pursue, or a new product or market bet | All ten 2006 questions, with strategy insights [S14][S23] |
| A team framing a problem it has been assigned | The four 2017 questions, then move quickly to solution discovery [S17] |
| A larger effort with several customer problems or business objectives | Customer letter (or press release) in addition [S19] |
| A very short frame | The three 2012–2013 questions, the intermediate form between the ten and the four [S15][S16] (plugin judgment, not in the sources) |
| An internal tool or service | The same sets (plugin judgment, not in the sources). Caveat (synthesis): Cagan's guidance is written mostly for commercial products; internal adaptations are secondary [S13] |
| A brand-new business with distribution and monetization open | A canvas, not these sets; outside this plugin's v1 [S16] |

Whichever set: if there is no product strategy, say so with the flag in `guardrails.md`; and the sets prompt thinking, they do not decide.
